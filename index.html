Hypothesis-Driven Clinical Reasoning

Case: Lateral Knee Pain

A 28-year-old recreational runner presents with progressive lateral knee pain for 3 months. No trauma. Pain is worse with downhill running and prolonged sitting.

What is your leading hypothesis right now?

A Iliotibial band syndrome (ITBS)
B Lateral meniscus tear
C Patellofemoral pain syndrome
D L4-5 radiculopathy

Next best examination priority?

A Ober’s test + hip abductor strength + gait observation
B McMurray’s test only
C Straight leg raise bilaterally

Reasoning Pathway

1. Pattern Recognition

Activity-related lateral knee pain in a runner → high pre-test probability of ITBS.

2. Red Flag Screen

No trauma, no night pain, no neurological signs → musculoskeletal primary.

3. Hypothesis Ranking

ITBS > PFPS > meniscal > referred lumbar.

4. Targeted Testing

Select tests with best likelihood ratios for the leading hypothesis.

5. Intervention Match

Load management + hip strength + running form before imaging.

Special Test Selection — Clinical Efficiency

Hypothesis: ACL deficiency

Patient is a 19-year-old soccer player 2 days post non-contact pivot injury. Moderate effusion, limited ROM, positive sense of instability. Select the highest-yield combination of tests (max 3).

Lachman Test

Highest sensitivity for ACL. Gold-standard clinical test.

Anterior Drawer

Useful but lower sensitivity in acute setting with hamstring guarding.

Pivot Shift

High specificity. May be limited by pain/guarding acutely.

McMurray Test

Meniscal test — lower priority given instability mechanism.

Valgus Stress (0° & 30°)

Rule out concurrent MCL involvement.

Straight Leg Raise

Neurological screen — low yield for this mechanism.

Clinical Decision Support
